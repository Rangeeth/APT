Course: Personal Training Certification

Topic: Scheduling, part 2 of 2 (analysing and designing a workout split)

About these notes: Short revision version of the Scheduling 2 lecture. For the full explanation of every concept, example and class question, see the Detailed Notes. Sources: the lecture recording, the course slides ("Scheduling"), the board photos and the published graphs shown in class.

Big idea: The number of main sets per muscle, the recovery needed, and how often the muscle is trained all fit together. Fewer sets work if you train the muscle more often, because each set adds less (diminishing returns) and the stimulus fades unless the muscle is trained again. Full body, upper/lower and PPL/bro splits are three frequency categories, and any training level can use any of them if the set range is respected.

3. Training level and F and I

  • F is proportional to 1/I: when intensity is low, frequency can be high, and vice versa.
  • Beginners train at low intensity (15 to 20 RM), so high frequency is fine.
  • Beginners also need high frequency to build neural pathways, the kinetic chain, proprioception, grip and tightness, and the Valsalva breathing technique. Training a muscle once a week would stretch the 2 to 3 month beginner phase.
  • Hence full body, three days a week, is best for beginners. The belief that full body is only for beginners is a myth: intermediate and advanced lifters can use it too, with the correct set range.

  • The unit is arbitrary (AU). The lecture shows it as rupees to make it easier to relate to.
  • "Added by this set" is the difference between consecutive totals (for example 1.61 minus 1.39 = 0.22).
  • Six sets are needed to double the stimulus of one set. Sets 2 to 6 together add about 1.00, the same as set 1.
  • Beyond six sets: 7 sets about 2.09, 8 about 2.16, 9 about 2.23.
  • Muscle damage rises with more sets and pulls the net stimulus into an inverted U. This is why there are upper limits (max 3, 5, 10). More easily damaged muscles turn down earlier.

5. Factor 2: stimulus retention

  • Stimulus is not stored forever. If the muscle is not trained again, it reaches zero on day (sets + 4), counting the training day as day 1.
  • 1 set: day 5. 2 sets: day 6. 3 sets: day 7. 4 sets: day 8. 5 sets: day 9. 6 sets: day 10.
  • Each extra set buys about one more day.
  • This applies to every level; only the weight and the person differ.

6. Linking sets and frequency

  • Low frequency with few sets means the stimulus is at zero before the next session, so nothing is carried forward.
  • High frequency lets you keep the ball in the air with few sets per session.
  • Lower frequency, more sets. Higher frequency, fewer sets.

7. Factor 3: determinants of frequency

  • Hypertrophy is growth of muscle cell size. Atrophy is shrinkage.
  • GAS is general adaptation syndrome. MyoPS is myofibrillar protein synthesis (building the contractile proteins myosin and actin inside the myofibril).
  • Hypertrophy (MyoPS) continues for the first 48 hours after training. After that, atrophy begins.
  • Ball analogy: shoot the ball up again before it hits the ground.
  • Atrophy days per week in the figure: 5 (once a week), 3 (twice a week), 1 (three times a week).
  • FBW (3x): the 48 hour window ends exactly on the next training day, so almost no atrophy occurs during the week.
  • U/L (2x): the muscle is trained again on day 4, before zero (day 7).
  • PPL/bro (1x): 6 sets last until day 10, and the next session (day 8) carries some balance forward.

The studies in one view

  • Recovery time rises roughly in line with the number of sets: up to 3 sets about 48 h, 4 to 5 sets about 72 h, 10 to 12 sets about a week.
  • Muscles differ: the pectoralis major, biceps and triceps recover slowest (more easily damaged). Quadriceps and calves recover fastest.
  • Maintenance research: 3 sets once a week gives no gain and no loss; about 0.32 units are lost per day after the 48 h window.
  • The figures are redrawn from the published charts (values approximate).

8. Sample programs (main sets only)

  • Samples are not to be copied. Real programs are built around the client's goals, strengths, weaknesses, likes, time, preferred frequency and medical concerns. No program is "best" unless made for the specific client.
  • Do 1 or 2 warm-up sets before the main sets.
  • Count synergists: biceps work in rows and pulldowns, triceps work in presses. Frequency counts every exercise where the muscle works.
  • A dedicated upper day gives room to hit all heads of the pectoralis major (flat for sternocostal and abdominal, incline for clavicular) and to add isolation.

11. Where the sources differ

  1. Reps. The lecture mentions 4 to 6 RM stimulating reps; the slides give 10 to 15 RM (intermediate) and 6 to 8 RM (advanced).
  2. PPL. The board shows PPL once a week (matching the slides' triple split). A six-day PPL trains each muscle twice, which by the lecture's rule would be the twice a week category (inference, not stated in the lecture).
  3. Sum of sets 2 to 6. Said as "roughly 1.02"; the exact sum is 1.00.
  4. Study names and days. Some were unclear in the recording; those in the detailed notes are read from the charts and are approximate.
  5. Lecture ended early. The PPL and bro samples and the final level column were not delivered; the level table above comes from the slides.
  6. E-book samples differ from the lecture's samples and rep ranges. Check with your course team.

13. Answers

  1. Main sets per muscle per session (not warm-up sets, not all exercises together).
  2. 1 to 3 sets: 48 h, thrice a week. 4 to 5: 72 h, twice. More than 5: 1 week, once.
  3. Frequency is counted per muscle. Each muscle is trained twice even though there are four WT days.
  4. Full body, upper/lower, and PPL or bro split.
  5. By movement pattern: pull (back, biceps), push (chest, shoulders, triceps), and legs.
  6. One body part a day. Shoulders are kept apart so the joint is not used on back-to-back days.
  7. F is proportional to 1/I: high frequency goes with low intensity.
  8. Neural pathways, the kinetic chain, proprioception, grip and tightness, and the Valsalva technique (any three).
  9. Low intensity allows high frequency, which speeds the learning phase. Intermediate and advanced lifters also gain with full body if they follow the set range.
  10. 1.00 for one set; 1.39 for two.
  11. 1.61 minus 1.39 = 0.22.
  12. Six sets (2.00).
  13. Each extra set adds less stimulus than the previous one, even though the total rises.
  14. Muscle damage rises with sets and is deducted from the stimulus.
  15. Day (sets + 4), with the training day as day 1.
  16. Higher frequency retrains the muscle before the stimulus reaches zero, so fewer sets are enough; once a week must use more sets to carry some balance forward.
  17. 4.17, 3.22 and 2.00.
  18. General adaptation syndrome and myofibrillar protein synthesis; about 48 hours.
  19. The day atrophy would begin is the day of the next session, so almost none occurs.
  20. 3x a week: 1 to 2 (max 3). 2x: 2 to 3 (max 5). 1x: 6 to 8 (not above 10).
